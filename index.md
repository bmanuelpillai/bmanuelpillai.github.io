# My Philosophy of Coding as a Researcher

*Coding as a Tool for Research*

## The Motivation to Learn to Code as a Researcher

### The Start Is The Hardest Part

When you first start to program it can feel really overwhelming. You aren't comfortable with the language, you have to look up everything before you can do what you want. You'll see more experienced programmers doing things a lot faster and in ways you've never seen before. It can be feel really overwhelming, but rest assured this will be
the hardest part of your programming journey. Once you gain experience and confidence as a programmer, you will feel like you can tackle any problem.

## The Doors Coding Opens for Researchers

Developing your coding skills opens a lot of doors to you when you go to analyze your data. If you have lots of data, you probably will not be able to run your analysis on your local computer due to hardware constraints. Learning some basics of using computing clusters or cloud computing will allow you to tackle this problem. Also, a lot of statistical methods such as bayesian inference require a lot of computation power.

## Why Coding is Fun

Coming from a STEM background one of my favorite parts about programming is how you get instant feedback from
running your code. Either your code runs or you get an error message. Every time your code runs you get a feeling of
accomplishment that drives you to over come the next bug in your code. Every time you squash a bug in your code, you improve your ability to diagnose and fix the next one.

One of my favorite parts of coding is the community of developers and researchers that are there to support you with their online material. You can look at other people's projects or online tutorials for inspiration. I highly recommend looking for course websites that professors post online as they are tailored for researchers and often include homework assignments to reinforce your learning. Most importantly, you learn by doing so pick projects you enjoy doing.

## Forest vs. Trees

Don't mistake the forest for the trees. I think coding is especially fun for scientists because it isn't our main job. We use code as a tool to answer the
questions that interest us, but our goal is not to make the most optimized or well-written. You may happen to become
really good at coding as a byproduct of tackling your research questions, coding is just a mean to reach that goal.
Write code first, then optimize it later if needed. Now, that is not to say that the code shouldn't be readable -- it
should still be well documented so that others can work on it too.

I include a lot of tools that make coding a lot easier and fun, but when you are first starting focus on getting
the basics down. This comes with time and practice. I recommend following one course and then focusing most of your time on projects.
